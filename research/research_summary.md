## 1. Exam structure and requirements (Virginia + NCSC Consortium)

### The two exams (NCSC Consortium)
- **Written exam** — 135 multiple-choice questions, all in English, closed book. Time limit **2 hours 15 minutes**.
  Passing score **80%** (at least 108 of 135 correct). Created by the National Center for State Courts (NCSC)
  and administered in Virginia by the Office of the Executive Secretary (OES) of the Supreme Court of Virginia,
  **twice a year**. Passing the written exam does **not** make a person a certified interpreter; it is one of
  several requirements.
- **Written exam content (three parts)** — Part 1: English Language / General Language Proficiency,
  **75 questions** (sentence completion, synonyms in context, synonyms, antonyms, idioms — high-register
  vocabulary, GRE/SAT-level words). Part 2: Court-Related Terms & Usage, **50 questions** (sentence completion
  with courtroom language, court-related questions, sequence-of-events questions; terms drawn largely from
  criminal court). Part 3: Ethics & Professional Conduct, **10 questions** (professional-conduct questions and
  ethical-dilemma scenarios). The idiom section is described by the Washington overview as "one of the biggest
  challenges for many test candidates."
- **Oral exam** — tests the **three modes of interpreting**: (a) sight translation (English ↔ non-English
  language, two portions); (b) consecutive interpretation (English ↔ non-English); (c) simultaneous
  interpretation (English → non-English only). Pass: **at least 70% on each section**, with **no less than 65%
  on either of the two sight translation portions**. Administered in person in Virginia, twice a year.
  Some certifiable languages have **abbreviated exams** that do not test all three modes (in Virginia:
  Bosnian-Croatian-Serbian and Turkish). Oral exam timing (Nebraska/NJ guidance): sight translation 6 minutes
  total (2 minutes prep); consecutive ~22 minutes (NJ: most finish in under 16 minutes; max passage 50 words;
  two repeats allowed, no clarification questions); simultaneous ~8–10 minutes. The whole oral exam takes
  about one hour; recordings are scored by at least two trained raters. Scores take weeks to months (one
  candidate reported 3 months).
- **Oral exam attempts (Virginia, Step 5 PDF):** three attempts allowed, once every six months. After three
  unsuccessful attempts, the candidate must complete a judicial interpreter training course (e.g. Interpretrain,
  Athena Sky, Edgar Hidalgo, De La Mora Institute, or a university course) at their own expense before testing
  again. Each exam version may be attempted twice. Oral exam fees in Virginia are language-dependent, $225–$600.

### Virginia certification process (six steps)
1. **Webinar series** — three one-hour webinars on the Virginia Judicial System (must complete before Step 2).
2. **Oral Proficiency Interview (OPI)** — speaking and listening scores in **both** English and the
   non-English language(s).
3. **Written exam** — 135 MCQs, 80% minimum, to be eligible for the 2-day orientation.
4. **2-day interpreter orientation + court observation + interview** — orientation covers the VA Judicial
   System, courtroom protocol, the interpreter's role, the modes of interpreting, and a thorough review of the
   Code of Professional Responsibility, plus self-study techniques and oral-exam prep. Then **15 hours of
   courtroom observation**, a resume documenting relevant interpreting experience, and an interview with a
   member of the Certification Team (courtroom observation experience, prior interpreting experience,
   understanding of ethical responsibilities).
5. **Oral exam** — the three modes, 70%/65% thresholds above.
6. **Sign the Interpreter's Oath** — agreeing to adhere to the Code of Professional Responsibility for
   Foreign Language Interpreters in Virginia.

### Virginia designations (from October 2025)
- **Conditionally Approved** — first four steps complete + at least **50% on all sections** of the oral exam +
  resume + interview + oath. Authorized to interpret in Virginia's **lower courts**. Two years to progress to
  the next designation.
- **Non-Certified** — first four steps + at least **60% on all sections** of the oral exam + resume + interview
  + oath. Two years to pass the full oral certification exam. When assigning interpreters, preference goes to
  Non-Certified over Conditionally Approved; **Certified interpreters receive priority over all other designations.**
- **Certified** — first four steps + **70%+ on each of the three oral sections, no less than 65% on either
  sight translation** + resume + interview + oath. Renewal and continuing-education requirements to be
  announced in 2026.
- Transition period: non-certified interpreters already serving in Virginia courts have until **January 30, 2027**
  to comply with the new requirements. Candidates who do not actively pursue the milestones may be removed from
  the Virginia Interpreter Roster.
- **Certified vs. Qualified status:** "Certified" is for languages with a certification exam; "Qualified" is
  for languages with **no** certification exam (same Steps 1–4, no oral certification exam).
- **Certifiable languages in Virginia (19):** Amharic, Arabic (Egyptian), Arabic (Levantine),
  Bosnian-Croatian-Serbian, Cantonese, Filipino (Tagalog), French, Haitian Creole, Hmong, Khmer, Korean,
  **Mandarin**, Polish, Portuguese, Russian, Somali, Spanish, Turkish, Vietnamese. (The program page says
  "18 languages" in one reciprocity paragraph but lists 19; the certification-requirements page lists the same
  19. Count discrepancy noted — see §6.)
- **Reciprocity:** Virginia offers certification reciprocity (for the certifiable languages) and qualification
  reciprocity (other languages) under standards set by the Office of the Executive Secretary.
- **Directory:** OES maintains a public directory of certified interpreters who agreed to share contact info
  for **private** interpretation services outside the court system; for court-related matters, contact the
  clerk's office where the case will be heard. Court forms must be filed in English; translated forms are
  informational only.

### NCSC / Consortium background
- The **National Center for State Courts** (Williamsburg, VA) is the developer of the national court
  interpreter examinations and the home of the **Consortium for Language Access in the Courts** (formerly the
  Consortium for Court Interpreter Certification). Most states use Consortium exams; specific requirements vary
  by state. NCSC also helps courts create or expand language access programs.
- The **Language Access Advisory Committee (LAAC)** is a subcommittee of the Council of Language Access
  Coordinators (CLAC), established by the Conference of Chief Justices and the Conference of State Court
  Administrators (COSCA); it focuses on language access issues and initiatives.
- Related credentials (for contrast, not Virginia's route): the **Federal Court Interpreter Certification
  Examination (FCICE)** is Spanish-only and is widely described as one of the hardest professional exams in the
  field (pass rate under 20%; 75% required in both English and Spanish sections); **NAJIT** also offers a
  Spanish-only certification exam. Some states recognize FCICE/NAJIT/NCSC interchangeably.
- Pass-rate context (NJ Courts, 1987–2016 data): across all languages, only **~12% of candidates pass** the
  full oral exam; per-language pass rates ranged from 0% to 21%. The **simultaneous section is consistently the
  most difficult mode** for candidates in every language. NCSC does not publish an exam-wide pass rate for the
  written exam.

## 2. Court structure and procedure (ABA "How Courts Work")

### Court structure
- Two distinct systems in every state: **state courts and federal courts**. Over **95% of cases** are handled
  by state courts (traffic, divorce, wills/estates, property).
- Three layers in both systems: **trial courts** (where cases start) → **intermediate appellate courts**
  (most appeals first heard) → **courts of last resort** (usually called supreme courts; final authority).
- Courts interpret and apply the law to specific controversies, resolve disputes, uphold limits on government,
  and protect minorities and people who cannot protect themselves. Judicial independence means cases are decided
  according to law and facts, not political currents.

### Civil vs. criminal cases
- **Civil cases:** conflicts between people or institutions (divorce/custody/child support, contracts,
  tort/personal injury e.g. auto collisions). The plaintiff files a **complaint**; the defendant answers.
  The plaintiff must prove liability by a **preponderance of the evidence** (the greater weight of the evidence).
- **Criminal cases:** the **government** prosecutes individuals/institutions for violating public codes of
  behavior. The government must prove guilt **beyond a reasonable doubt**. The defendant never has to prove
  innocence (or non-liability in civil cases).
- The same event can produce separate civil and criminal cases in the U.S. (unlike many countries, they are not
  combined).

### Criminal case flow
- **Felonies** (robbery, assault with a deadly weapon, sexual assault): punishment on conviction is imprisonment
  of **at least one year**, usually in a state/federal penitentiary. **Misdemeanors** (simple assault, DWI,
  trespassing): less than a year, usually in a local jail. Traffic infractions and petty misdemeanors are
  usually punished by fines only.
- Charges are brought three ways: (1) **indictment** voted by a **grand jury**; (2) **information** filed by
  the prosecuting attorney (or a criminal **complaint** by an individual); (3) **citation** by a police officer
  for minor offenses. The charge must state the time, date, place, the accused's alleged involvement, and the
  crime's details.
- **Grand jury:** a group (traditionally 23) summoned to determine whether the prosecutor's facts and accusations
  warrant an indictment and trial; exists in the federal system and almost all states, but only about half the
  states require grand juries for felony charges. Grand juries also investigate public corruption and monitor
  jail conditions in many states.
- **Arrest:** when taken into custody, before questioning, the person must be told of **Miranda rights** (U.S.
  Supreme Court, *Miranda v. Arizona*, 1966): anything said may be used against them; right to remain silent;
  right to consult a lawyer before and during questioning; right to an appointed lawyer if they cannot afford
  one. A person arrested without a warrant generally may not be held more than **48 hours** without an initial
  appearance/arraignment before a judge or magistrate.
- **Pre-trial appearances:** misdemeanors — initial appearance/arraignment (charge read, penalties explained,
  rights advised incl. counsel; indigent defendants get appointed counsel; plea entered; bail set). A
  **nolo contendere** ("no contest") plea is equivalent to a guilty plea in many jurisdictions except the
  defendant does not directly admit guilt. Felonies add a **preliminary hearing** — the government must show
  **probable cause** that the defendant committed the crime; if none, the matter is dismissed; if found, the
  defendant is **bound over** to trial court. At the felony initial appearance the defendant does **not** enter
  a plea.
- **Bail:** money defendants post to be released until trial. **Bail is not a fine and not punishment**; its
  purpose is to ensure appearance at trial and pretrial hearings. It is returned when the trial is over (minus
  a processing fee in some states). The judge/magistrate weighs flight risk, type of crime, dangerousness, and
  community safety. Defendants may be released on their **own recognizance** (no payment, promise to appear).
  Bail bondspersons typically charge ~10% of the bond.
- **Plea bargaining:** resolves **most** criminal cases in most jurisdictions. The defendant typically pleads
  guilty to a lesser charge or one of several charges, or pleads as charged with the prosecution recommending
  leniency — but **the judge is not bound** by the prosecution's recommendation. Many plea bargains need court
  approval. **Diversion** programs remove less serious matters from formal procedure (probation without trial;
  successful completion can lead to **expungement**).

### Civil pre-trial procedure
- Suit begins with filing a **complaint** (plaintiff's version of facts, legal theory, damages/relief sought);
  a **summons** notifies the defendant, who files an **answer** admitting or denying allegations. In domestic
  relations the parties are **petitioner** and **respondent**.
- **Jurisdiction:** a court can decide a case only if it has authority over the subject matter and over the
  person or property involved. **Venue** is the county/district where the lawsuit is tried; it can be changed
  (e.g. for prejudicial pretrial publicity or witness convenience). Transitory actions can be brought wherever
  the defendant is found and served; local actions (e.g. foreclosure) only where the subject is located.
- **Pleadings:** complaint (or petition/bill), answer, reply, **counterclaim** (defendant sues plaintiff back).
- **Motions** (requests for a legal ruling, not pleadings): motion to discover, **motion to dismiss** (suit has
  no legally sound basis even if all facts alleged are true), **motion for summary judgment** (judgment before
  trial where there is no dispute about the facts and only a question of law remains).
- **Discovery:** formal exchange of information about witnesses and evidence to prevent "trial by ambush."
  Methods: **depositions** (out-of-court statements under oath, usable at trial or to discredit a witness whose
  trial testimony differs), **interrogatories** (written questions answered in writing under oath),
  subpoenas for documents, physical examinations, and document-authentication requests.
- Most civil cases **settle** by mutual agreement — before filing, before trial, during trial, during
  deliberations, or even after a verdict. A settlement usually states no one was right or wrong and need not
  settle the whole case. Pre-trial conferences help narrow issues and encourage settlement.

### Trial
- **Officers of the court:** the **judge** presides, rules on points of law, instructs the jury (in a bench
  trial the judge also decides the facts and verdict); the **court clerk/bailiff** administers oaths and manages
  exhibits; the **bailiff** keeps order, calls witnesses, and is in charge of the jury (ensuring no one
  influences it); the **court reporter** records **verbatim** everything said in formal proceedings (testimony,
  objections, rulings); **lawyers** for both sides are officers of the court who represent clients zealously
  within the Code of Professional Conduct.
- **Jury selection:** juries of **6–12** (civil often 6; serious criminal cases generally 12). **Voir dire**
  ("to speak the truth") is the questioning of prospective jurors about knowledge of the case or bias.
  **Challenges for cause** (unlimited; e.g. juror is a relative of a party) vs. **peremptory challenges**
  (limited number; no cause stated; cannot be used to discriminate on race or sex). Alternate jurors hear
  evidence but deliberate only if they replace an original juror.
- **Opening statements:** confined to facts to be proved by the evidence; cannot be argumentative. The party
  with the **burden of proof** (government in criminal, plaintiff in civil) goes first.
- **Evidence:** two types — **direct evidence** (speaks for itself: eyewitness accounts, a confession, a weapon)
  and **circumstantial evidence** (suggests a fact by implication/inference: crime-scene appearance, physical
  evidence suggesting criminal activity). **Neither kind is more valuable than the other.** Strict rules govern
  admissibility.
- **Direct examination:** the calling lawyer questions their own witness; **leading questions are generally not
  allowed**; witnesses generally cannot state opinions unless qualified as **expert witnesses**. **Objections**
  require a specific legal reason; the judge **sustains** or **overrules**. A ruling is not taking sides.
- **Cross-examination:** limited to matters raised on direct; **leading questions are allowed** (to test
  credibility; also allowed for adverse/hostile witnesses on direct). Counsel may **impeach** (question/reduce
  credibility) by showing bias, interest in the outcome, or prior felony/moral-turpitude convictions.
- **Closing arguments (summations):** discuss the evidence and proper inferences only — no issues outside the
  case or unpresented evidence. Plaintiff/government first, then defense; the burden-bearing side gets a final
  **rebuttal**. If the defense waives closing, the other side loses its second argument.
- **Jury instructions (the judge's charge):** the judge states the issues, defines unfamiliar terms, explains
  the standard of proof, and tells jurors they are the sole judges of facts and witness credibility; opening and
  closing arguments are **not evidence**. Jurors must follow the judge's statement of the law.
- **Deliberations:** the jury elects a **foreperson** (presiding juror); the bailiff ensures no one communicates
  with the jury; questions go to the judge by note. Criminal verdicts must usually be **unanimous**; some states
  allow less-than-unanimous civil verdicts; all federal cases require unanimity. A jury that cannot agree is a
  **hung jury**, leading to a **mistrial**; the case may be retried before a new jury. Jurors may be
  **sequestered** (housed in a hotel, secluded from outside contact) if deliberations run past the day.
- **Mistrials** (trials terminated and declared void before verdict): death of a juror or attorney, impropriety
  in drawing the jury, fundamental prejudicial error that cannot be cured by instructions, juror misconduct
  (contact with a party, considering outside evidence, independent investigation), or a hopelessly deadlocked jury.
- **Verdict:** announced by the foreperson or court clerk. Criminal: guilty or not guilty. Civil: for plaintiff
  or defendant (plus damages, often after a separate hearing). Either lawyer may ask that the jury be
  **polled** — each juror asked if they agree with the announced verdict.
- **Judgment:** the jury's decision takes effect only when the judge enters judgment (an order filed in public
  records). In civil cases the judge may increase/decrease damages; in criminal cases the judge generally cannot
  modify the verdict. Unpaid civil damages may be collected by **execution** (sheriff seizes and sells property).
- **Sentencing:** after conviction, a **pre-sentence investigation** (prior record, family, health, work record)
  helps the judge choose within the statutory range. In most states and federal courts **only the judge**
  determines the sentence; the main exception is jury sentencing where the death penalty is possible. Federal
  courts and some states use **sentencing guidelines** for uniformity.
- **Appeals:** not automatic — there must be a legal basis (an alleged material error), not mere dislike of the
  verdict. In civil cases either party may appeal; in criminal cases **only the defendant** in most states
  (prosecution appeals after a verdict are barred by **double jeopardy**). An appeal is **not a retrial** — no
  new witnesses or evidence; it argues errors of procedure or law. The appealing party is the **appellant**
  (petitioner); the other side the **appellee** (respondent). Process: **notice of appeal** → appellant's
  **brief** → appellee's answering brief → optional **oral argument** (U.S. Supreme Court: ~1 hour total;
  federal appeals courts: often 10–15 minutes per side). The court reverses only for error of law;
  **harmless errors** (no effect on the outcome) do not cause reversal; **reversible errors** do. Opinions:
  **majority**, **dissenting**, **concurring**, or unsigned **per curiam**. If reversed, the case is usually
  **remanded** (new trial, modified judgment, or reconsideration). In civil cases an appeal does not ordinarily
  stay enforcement unless the appellant posts a **supersedeas bond**. State-convicted defendants who exhaust
  state appeals may file **habeas corpus** in federal court alleging federal constitutional violations.

## 3. Key terminology (NCSC Consortium Glossary, Feb 2011)

The glossary contains **856 entries** (extracted in full). It was compiled by the Consortium for Language
Access in the Courts as a core English base for bilingual glossaries and as a source document for technical
terms on Consortium oral performance exams. Selected high-frequency terms (definitions condensed from the
glossary; the question bank draws on these):

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Accomplice | One who knowingly, voluntarily, and intentionally unites with the principal offender in a crime; a partner in a crime. |
| Accessory | A person who aids or contributes in the commission of a crime. |
| Affidavit | A written statement of facts confirmed by the oath of the party making it, before a notary or officer authorized to administer oaths. |
| Alibi | A defense claim that the accused was somewhere else at the time a crime was committed. |
| Alford plea | A guilty plea in which the defendant does not admit guilt but concedes the State has sufficient evidence to convict (North Carolina v. Alford, 400 U.S. 25 (1970)). |
| Arraignment | Proceeding in which the accused is brought before the court to plead to the criminal charge; the charge is read and the accused pleads guilty, not guilty, or (where permitted) nolo contendere. |
| Bail | Money or other security provided to the court to temporarily allow release from jail and assure appearance in court. |
| Bench trial | Trial without a jury in which a judge decides the facts. |
| Beyond a reasonable doubt | The criminal standard: the jury must be satisfied to a moral certainty that every element of a crime has been proven; absolute certainty is not required. |
| Burden of proof | The obligation of a party to establish by evidence a requisite degree of belief in the mind of the trier of fact. |
| Concurrent sentences | Sentences for more than one crime served at the same time. |
| Consecutive sentences | Successive sentences, one beginning at the expiration of another. |
| Contempt of court | A finding that an act was committed with intent to embarrass the court, disobey its lawful orders, or obstruct justice. |
| Deposition | A pretrial discovery device: one party questions the other party or a witness under oath, usually in a lawyer's office before a court reporter. |
| Double jeopardy | The Fifth Amendment prohibition against being put on trial more than once for the same offense. |
| Felony | A crime more serious than a misdemeanor, usually punishable by imprisonment in a penitentiary for more than a year and/or substantial fines. |
| Grand jury | Jury of inquiry; determines which charges, if any, are to be brought against a defendant. |
| Hearsay | Statements by a witness who did not see or hear the incident but heard about it from someone else; usually not admissible. |
| Indictment | A formal written accusation issued by a grand jury charging a party with a crime. |
| Jurisdiction | (1) The legal authority of a court to hear and decide a case. (2) The geographic area over which the court has authority. |
| Misdemeanor | A lesser offense than a felony, generally punishable by fine or limited jail time, not in a penitentiary. |
| Perjury | A false statement given while under oath or in a sworn affidavit. |
| Plaintiff | A person who initiates a lawsuit against another (also called the complainant). |
| Preponderance of the evidence | Evidence of greater weight or more convincing than the evidence offered in opposition; the civil standard. |
| Probable cause | A reasonable belief that a crime has been or is being committed; the basis for all lawful searches, seizures, and arrests. |
| Probation | A sentence releasing a convicted offender into the community under conditions and supervision of a probation officer instead of incarceration; violation can lead to revocation and imprisonment. |
| Subpoena | A court order requiring a person to be present at a certain time and place to give testimony; failure to appear may be punished as contempt. |
| Venue | The proper geographical area (county, city, or district) in which a court with jurisdiction may hear a case. |
| Verdict | The opinion of a jury (or judge where there is no jury) on the factual issues of a case. |
| Voir dire | "To speak the truth": preliminary examination of prospective jurors by court and attorneys to determine qualification and suitability. |
| Warrant | Most commonly, a court order authorizing law enforcement to make an arrest or conduct a search; the supporting affidavit must establish probable cause. |

Other glossary terms used in the question bank: admission, adversary system, affirmative defense, alternative
dispute resolution (ADR), appeal, challenge for cause, circumstantial evidence, complaint, conviction,
cross-examination, defendant, discovery, hung jury, mistrial, motion, parole, plea bargain, prosecutor, public
defender, restitution, sentence, summons, testimony, acquittal.

## 4. Interpreter ethics — VA Code of Professional Responsibility (10 Canons)

The Code of Professional Responsibility for Interpreters Serving in Virginia Courts (page last modified
June 21, 2002) has ten canons. Candidates sign the Interpreter's Oath agreeing to adhere to it (Step 6).

1. **Accuracy and completeness** — Render a complete and accurate interpretation or sight translation, **without
   altering, omitting, or adding anything** and **without explanation**. Twofold duty: (a) English proceedings
   must reflect precisely what the non-English speaker said; (b) place the non-English speaker on an equal
   footing. Preserve the meaning **including the style or register** of speech. Verbatim/"word for word"
   interpretation is **not** appropriate when it distorts meaning — but **every** spoken statement must be
   interpreted, even if non-responsive, obscene, rambling, incoherent, or an apparent misstatement. Never
   interject one's own words; if an explanation of an interpreting problem is needed, **ask the court's
   permission**. Convey the speaker's emotional emphasis without reenacting or mimicking emotions or gestures.
   **Duty to correct any error of interpretation** discovered during the proceeding. Objectively analyze any
   challenge to one's performance.
2. **Representation of qualifications** — Accurately and completely represent certifications, training, and
   pertinent experience, **before** appointment, so officers of the court can fairly evaluate qualifications.
   Accepting a case conveys linguistic competency in legal settings; withdrawing mid-case disrupts proceedings
   and wastes public resources.
3. **Impartiality and avoidance of conflict of interest** — Be impartial and unbiased; refrain from conduct
   giving an **appearance of bias**; **disclose any real or perceived conflict of interest**. The interpreter
   serves as an **officer of the court**; the duty is to serve the court and the public, whether publicly or
   privately retained. Avoid favoritism, personal dependence of the non-English-speaking party, and casual
   personal conversations in court that could suggest a special relationship. Strive for professional detachment;
   avoid verbal and non-verbal displays of personal attitudes, prejudices, emotions, or opinions. If a
   participant views the interpreter as biased, disclose that to the judicial authority and counsel. **Presumed
   conflicts (should not serve):** (1) friend, associate, or relative of a party or counsel; (2) served in an
   investigative capacity for any party; (3) previously retained by a law enforcement agency to help prepare the
   criminal case at issue; (4) the interpreter (or spouse/child) has a financial or other interest affected by
   the outcome; (5) involved in the choice of counsel or law firm. Also: disclose prior private employment by a
   party; **never serve where payment is contingent on the outcome**; an interpreter who is also an attorney
   must not serve in both capacities in the same matter.
4. **Professional demeanor** — Conduct oneself consistent with the dignity of the court; be **as unobtrusive as
   possible**. Know and observe protocol; speak at a rate and volume audible throughout the courtroom; dress
   consistent with the dignity of the proceedings; avoid obstructing anyone's view; avoid conduct that could
   discredit the court.
5. **Confidentiality** — Protect the confidentiality of **all privileged and other confidential information**,
   especially **attorney-client privilege** and other privileged communications. Never repeat or disclose
   confidential information obtained in the course of employment. **Exception:** if the interpreter learns of
   information suggesting **imminent harm** to someone or a crime being committed during proceedings, immediately
   disclose it to an appropriate authority **within the judiciary who is not involved in the proceeding** and
   seek advice about the conflict in professional responsibility.
6. **Restriction of public comment** — Do not publicly discuss, report, or offer an opinion about a matter in
   which the interpreter is or has been engaged, **even when the information is not privileged or required by
   law to be confidential**.
7. **Scope of practice** — Limit oneself to interpreting/translating; **do not give legal advice, express
   personal opinions, or perform other services** while serving as interpreter. Do not initiate communications
   except as necessary for accurate interpretation (e.g., asking a speaker to slow down, repeat, or rephrase;
   correcting one's own errors; notifying the court of reservations about competence) — and when doing so, make
   clear one is speaking for oneself. An interpreter may convey legal advice from an attorney **only while that
   attorney is giving it**. May **translate the language on a form** but **may not explain the form or its
   purpose**. Must not perform official acts that belong to other court officials (clerks, pretrial release
   investigators, probation counselors).
8. **Assessing and reporting impediments to performance** — Continually assess one's ability to deliver services;
   **immediately convey any reservation** about competence to the appropriate judicial authority. Notify the
   court of: un-interpretable communication mode/language; environmental/physical limitations (room too noisy,
   multiple people speaking at once, speech too rapid); need for **periodic breaks** to prevent interpreter
   fatigue; recommend **team interpreting** when necessary. Ask about the nature of a case before accepting;
   **decline cases likely to exceed one's skills**. If unfamiliar technical terminology arises (e.g., unscheduled
   expert witness), **request a brief recess** to familiarize oneself; if extensive research is needed, inform
   the presiding officer. Notify the presiding officer of any **personal bias** (e.g., a sexual-assault victim
   interpreting a similar case may wish to be excused).
9. **Duty to report ethical violations** — Report to the proper judicial authority any effort to impede
   compliance with any law, this code, or official policy governing court interpreting. First inform the person
   of one's professional obligations; if they persist, turn to a supervisory interpreter, a judge, or another
   official with jurisdiction over interpreter matters.
10. **Professional development** — Continually improve skills and knowledge and advance the profession through
    training, education, and interaction with colleagues and specialists; keep informed of statutes, court rules,
    and judiciary policies; stay current on technical, vernacular, and regional terminology.

Related exam-style scenario guidance (WA Consortium overview): when an expert witness gives highly technical
testimony the defendant may not understand, the interpreter should **interpret at the same level as the
witness** — not summarize, simplify, or explain. At the witness stand, keep dictionary and notepad at hand.

## 5. Language-access concepts

- **Limited English proficiency (LEP):** the population the system serves. The mission of Virginia's Foreign
  Language Services Division is "to assist individuals with limited English proficiency in overcoming language
  barriers to ensure equal access to Virginia's Judicial System," by providing interpretation and sight
  translation services, certifying and hiring highly trained interpreters, and upholding professional standards.
- **Language access as equal access:** courts provide interpreters so LEP court users can participate on an
  equal footing with English speakers — the interpreter's twofold duty (Canon 1) operationalizes this.
- **The Consortium / NCSC role:** NCSC develops the national court interpreter examinations and supports courts
  building or expanding language access programs; the Consortium's glossary exists partly to standardize the
  technical terms used on oral exams across member states.
- **The three modes of interpreting:** (1) **sight translation** — reading a written document aloud in another
  language (e.g., a letter to a judge, police report, or contract; ~225 words per portion, 6 minutes);
  (2) **consecutive interpretation** — interpreting after the speaker pauses, using memory and notes
  (Q&A format, e.g. witness testimony; passages up to 50 words); (3) **simultaneous interpretation** —
  interpreting at the same time as the speaker, usually via headphones (English → non-English in the NCSC exam;
  ~8–10 minutes).
- **Interpreter as officer of the court:** the interpreter's duty runs to the court and the public, not to the
  party who pays — a core language-access principle (Canon 3 commentary).
- **Reciprocity:** states recognize one another's certifications under set standards, so a certified interpreter
  can work across Consortium member states without re-testing (Virginia offers both certification and
  qualification reciprocity).
- **Qualified vs. certified:** where no oral exam exists for a language, interpreters can still serve as
  "Qualified" after completing the webinar/OPI/written-exam/orientation steps — language access is not limited
  to the 19 certifiable languages.
- **Language access governance:** LAAC (Language Access Advisory Committee), a subcommittee of the Council of
  Language Access Coordinators under the Conference of Chief Justices / COSCA, coordinates national language
  access initiatives.

  reciprocity is available in "18 languages" but then lists 19 (Amharic, Arabic (Egyptian), Arabic (Levantine),
  Bosnian-Croatian-Serbian, Cantonese, Filipino (Tagalog), French, Haitian Creole, Hmong, Khmer, Korean,

  page lists the same 19 without giving a count. The question bank says "19 languages are listed" and names
  Mandarin as certifiable; the "18" figure is treated as a typo on the VA page.

  short landing page (LAAC description, services pitch, address). Exam specifics were corroborated from the VA
  program pages and other state court program pages (WA, RI, IL, NE, NJ, MI, OH, MD, CA) that administer the
  same NCSC Consortium exams. The written exam's 2h15m time limit and the 75/50/10 section split are NOT stated
  on the VA pages; they come from the Consortium-derived WA overview and are corroborated by RI/IL/NE pages.

  extractor, showed only Canon 3; the full 10-canon text was recovered by fetching the page HTML directly.

- **Reddit:** direct Reddit threads could not be retrieved — see reddit_findings.md. Community findings there
